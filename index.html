Thesis in progress

Radiation hardness of lead-free perovskite solar cells for space, with an MoS₂ electron transport layer

Undergraduate thesis · In progress · Supervisor: Md. Masud Parvez, Assistant Professor, Department of Science and Humanities, AAUB

Perovskite cells are efficient, low-cost and flexible, which makes them attractive for spacecraft. What is still unclear is how well they resist radiation and how stable they stay over a long mission. That gap is what the thesis addresses.

For a simple illustration of what radiation does to a solar cell, see the interactive J–V model in Background.

I use molybdenum disulfide (MoS₂) as the electron transport layer. Device performance is simulated in SCAPS-1D and the atomic structure of the materials is studied in Materials Studio. The radiation-effects stage uses SPENVIS, SR-NIEL and SRIM/TRIM, described below. The thesis radiation results are still to come, but I have already run this chain once, with teammates, in a course project (see Projects).

Radiation-effects tools

Selected for the radiation-effects stage of the thesis

I use SPENVIS for the particle environment of an orbit, SR-NIEL for the damage each particle causes, and SRIM/TRIM for where that damage lands in the device layers. I have run SPENVIS and SRIM/TRIM myself, with the MULASSIS shielding tool, in the machine-learning course project described under Projects.

About the three toolsHide details
SPENVIS
The Space Environment Information System, a web-based tool for the European Space Agency developed and maintained at BIRA-IASB since 1996. It models the radiation environment along an orbit (trapped proton and electron belts, solar energetic particles, cosmic rays) and the effect of shielding. For solar cells it offers an equivalent-fluence method (JPL, EQFLUX) and a displacement-damage-dose method (NRL, MC-SCREAM) that is based on NIEL.
SR-NIEL
A Screened Relativistic non-ionising energy loss calculator (Boschini, Rancoita and Tacconi), an analytical code that is also integrated into SPENVIS. NIEL is the part of a particle’s energy loss that goes into displacing atoms, so it allows damage from different particles and energies to be compared. It gives NIEL for electrons, protons and ions against particle energy and displacement threshold energy, and treats compounds with Bragg’s rule.
SRIM/TRIM
The Stopping and Range of Ions in Matter, whose core, TRIM (Transport of Ions in Matter), is a Monte Carlo simulation by J. F. Ziegler and co-workers that began in 1980. It follows ions through layered targets and gives their range, stopping and the vacancies they create. It treats the target as amorphous, has no crystal structure or annealing, and its full-cascade vacancy counts are known to overestimate damage, so the damage-energy or Kinchin–Pease options are recommended.

AeroSoar (MIT)

Machine-learning prediction of a proton-irradiated lead-free solar cell

Course project, semester 7 · with Md. Azharul Islam and Sajidur Rahman Hamim · supervisor Dr. S. M. Sadakatul Bari, AAUB · simulation only

We ran the radiation chain ourselves for the cell ITO/C60/Rb₂AgInBr₆/Cu₂NiSnS₄/Pt in the orbit of the International Space Station. SPENVIS (AP9 trapped-proton model, 365-day mission) gave the proton spectrum, MULASSIS gave the spectrum behind 5 mm of glass (3.08% of the forward fluence gets through), and SRIM/TRIM gave the vacancies each proton makes in the 1 µm absorber. For one representative energy bin this gave a radiation defect density of 1.59 × 10⁷ cm⁻³, added as a second defect in SCAPS-1D. It is negligible next to the intrinsic defects (it changes the open-circuit voltage and efficiency by about 10⁻⁶), so our report says a real radiation study must sum all 39 energy bins and use a higher fluence.

The same SCAPS-1D model produced 4,212 simulated devices (efficiency 1.5 to 37%) to train five regression models. CatBoost predicted efficiency with a test R² of 0.9999; on 48 devices outside the training grid the efficiency error was 0.48%, and SHAP analysis showed that defect density and acceptor density control open-circuit voltage, fill factor and efficiency. The model fails outside its range (R² of 0.54 when the highest defect densities are held out), and the report says so. The data are simulated, the cell is a different material from my thesis cell, and nothing is checked against experiment.

AeroSoar: vertical air velocity estimation for soaring gliders

Simulation study · JavaScript and Python · open source, MIT licence · first author, with Md. Azharul Islam and Sajidur Rahman Hamim

A simulation environment and set of experiments for a study of causal signal processing for estimating vertical air velocity and detecting thermals in autonomous soaring gliders. The simulator models the glider, the atmosphere and its sensors, and compares causal filters and detectors, including Kalman, extended Kalman and particle filters and CUSUM, under sensor faults and across different missions.

One result from the study: in simulation, substituting the true speed rate for the Kalman filter’s estimate cut its error during speed changes from 0.70 to 0.05 m/s, and a delay-matched version reached 0.26 m/s. Results are reproducible from fixed random seeds, and the repository includes the manuscript source and a map from each claim to its result file.

AeroSoar is a different field from the thesis but the same working method: a simulation with stated assumptions, limits written down, and results that others can rerun.

Recorded flights of a powered fixed-wing aircraft from the public ALFA dataset are used only to check the sensor assumptions. The work is simulation-only; flight readiness is not claimed, and validation on recorded thermal flights has not been done.

Solar panel selection for the PicoSat

PicoSat4Bangladesh · trade study

I compared three commercial 1U CubeSat solar panels on efficiency, power output, temperature range, sensors and price, then recommended one for the satellite.

Table 3. Candidate 1U CubeSat solar panels, as listed by the manufacturers. The highlighted row is the panel I recommended.

PanelEfficiencyPowerTemperature rangeListed price
Spectrolab XTE-SF32.2% (BOL)3–4.5 W per panel−120 to +100 °C≈ $2,500–3,000
Aphelion 1U solar (chosen)30.5%Up to 4.5 W per panel−40 to +125 °C$1,499
EnduroSat 1U Solar-Z29+%2.4+ W in LEONot listed$2,800–3,600 (with magnetorquer)

I recommended the Aphelion panel. It was the cheapest of the three, gives enough power for the CubeSat’s subsystems, integrates easily through standard connectors with built-in temperature and sun sensors, and is designed for missions of up to two years in LEO. Spectrolab’s higher efficiency and wider temperature range go beyond what a short educational LEO mission needs.
